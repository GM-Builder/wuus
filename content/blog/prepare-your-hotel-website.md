You do not need to arrive with a perfect brief. Start with the information a guest needs to understand your property, choose a room and contact you or book. This checklist helps you gather that information before design begins.

## 1. Decide what the website should help guests do

Choose the main action: send an inquiry, contact your property, or open your existing booking engine. If you already accept reservations through another service, share that link with your designer.

A website with a booking link and a website that manages live availability are different projects. Agree on which one you need. A visual booking demo does not confirm a reservation, take payment or check inventory.

## 2. Gather the facts about your property

Write a short introduction, the full address, check-in and check-out times, contact details and the languages your team can answer in. Include directions and any arrival instructions guests should know.

For each room type, collect:

- The room name, maximum occupancy and bed arrangement.
- Facilities and accessibility information you can confirm.
- Photos that match that room type.
- Any terms guests need before booking, including extra charges where applicable.

If a detail is uncertain, flag it for review. An attractive page should still describe what guests will actually find.

## 3. Choose photos you have permission to use

Gather original files rather than screenshots when possible. Cover the exterior, rooms, bathrooms, shared spaces and any facilities you want to feature. Check that the photos represent the property today.

Confirm permission with the photographer and anyone identifiable in the images. Do not assume that a photo can be reused just because it appears on a booking platform. If you need new photography, discuss that separately from website design.

## 4. Put the booking and contact journey in writing

Send the exact booking destination and the email address or phone number that should receive inquiries. Decide who will check incoming messages and how your team will respond.

Before launch, test the journey from a guest's perspective: open the site on a phone, choose a room, follow the booking link or send a test inquiry, then confirm that it reaches the intended destination. Record any steps your team still needs to complete.

## 5. Confirm scope, costs and ownership

Ask for a written proposal before paying a deposit. It should name the pages, room types, languages, revisions and delivery milestones included in the project.

Also clarify:

- Who pays for the domain, hosting and third-party services.
- Whether you can edit content yourself or request changes from the studio.
- What you receive at handover, including code and account access.
- What support covers after launch and which changes cost extra.

For a WUUS project, the agreed proposal defines your scope. Read the [project terms](/hospitality/terms) alongside it and ask about anything unclear.

## 6. Name one person to approve the work

Choose one contact to consolidate feedback. Collect comments into a single list per revision round so that changes do not conflict.

Keep approved text, photos and decisions in one shared folder. Tell your designer when the materials are ready; the production schedule should be agreed with that readiness in mind.

## Start with the materials you already have

A useful first folder contains property details, room information, approved photos, contact destinations and your booking link. Missing items can become a clear action list rather than a reason to postpone every conversation.
